SPF, DKIM and DMARC explained for agency owners

What SPF, DKIM and DMARC do, how to check any domain’s records, what Google and Yahoo require, and how to turn a weak result into a fair first email.

Three DNS records tell mail servers whether email from a domain is genuine: SPF, DKIM and DMARC. For an agency they matter twice: a prospect’s weak setup is a real problem you can verify and help fix, and Google and Yahoo expect the same records on your own outreach.

In short

  • SPF lists who may send for a domain, DKIM signs each message, and DMARC checks that one of them matches the From address.
  • All three are public DNS records: checking a domain sends it nothing.
  • Google and Yahoo require SPF or DKIM from every sender, and all three from bulk senders.
  • Warning signs: no SPF, more than 10 DNS lookups, no DMARC, or p=none that never moves.
  • Get your own domain right first, then point out one finding calmly, with the fix.

What SPF, DKIM and DMARC do

SPF: who may send

SPF (Sender Policy Framework, RFC 7208) is one TXT record on the domain that lists the servers allowed to send its email. Receivers look up the domain in the envelope sender, the return path most people never see, and check that the sending server is on its list.

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all is the record Google documents for a domain that sends only through Google Workspace. Google’s servers may send; mail from any other server is probably not allowed.

At the end, -all fails every other server, ~all marks them as probably not allowed (Google recommends it), ?all stays neutral, and +all lets anyone send as the domain.

-all has a catch: RFC 9989 warns that it can get mail refused before DMARC sees its DKIM signature. Whatever the ending, keep to one record: only one TXT record on a domain may start with v=spf1.

DKIM: a signature on each message

With DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail, RFC 6376), the sending service signs each message. The receiver fetches the public key from DNS and checks the signature, which also shows the signed parts weren’t changed on the way.

The key lives at selector._domainkey.yourdomain.ca, and Google Workspace’s default selector is google. DNS can’t list selectors: to find one, view a message’s source and read s= in its DKIM-Signature header, alongside d=, the signing domain.

DMARC: the rule that ties them together

DMARC (RFC 9989, which replaced RFC 7489 in May 2026) is a TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain.ca. It tells receivers what to do with mail that shows the domain in its From address but fails DMARC, and where to send reports.

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:reports@yourdomain.ca makes a good first record. Its policy, p=, takes one of three values:

  • none: no preference, only reports. RFC 9989 calls this monitoring mode
  • quarantine: treat failing mail as suspicious, which can mean the spam folder
  • reject: refuse failing mail

How they work together: alignment

SPF and DKIM each vouch for a domain, but not always the one people see. A scammer can pass SPF for a domain they own while showing yours in the From line. DMARC closes that gap with alignment.

A message passes DMARC when SPF or DKIM passes and the domain it passed for matches the From domain. With relaxed alignment, the default, a subdomain counts: news.yourdomain.ca aligns with yourdomain.ca. Strict alignment (adkim=s or aspf=s) needs the exact domain.

One aligned pass is enough, and RFC 9989 asks for two where possible. Lean on DKIM: forwarded mail will most likely fail SPF unless the forwarder rewrites the return path, while DKIM signatures generally survive.

When a sending service fails DMARC

If a newsletter or booking tool uses its own domain for the return path and for its DKIM signature, both checks can pass but neither matches your From domain, so DMARC fails. Set the tool to sign with your domain.

How to check any domain

It’s all public, so checking a domain sends nothing to its owner. The quickest way is our email check: type a domain and it reads MX, SPF, DKIM and DMARC from your browser in a few seconds, with what to fix.

By hand

  1. 1

    Look up the SPF record

    In a terminal, run nslookup -type=TXT domain.ca on Windows or dig +short TXT domain.ca on a Mac, and find the record that starts with v=spf1.

  2. 2

    Look up the DMARC record

    Do the same for _dmarc.domain.ca. No record starting with v=DMARC1 means no DMARC.

  3. 3

    Look up a DKIM key

    With the selector from a message’s s= value, look up selector._domainkey.domain.ca. No answer under a guessed name proves nothing.

What a weak result looks like

Act on these, on any domain, yours included.

What you seeWhat it means, and the fix
No SPF recordReceivers can’t tell who may send for the domain. Fix: publish one record listing every sender.
Two SPF recordsOnly one is allowed, so SPF returns an error. Fix: merge them into one.
+all at the endAny server in the world may send as the domain. Fix: end with ~all or -all.
Over 10 DNS lookupsSPF returns an error and can’t pass. Fix: remove what the domain no longer uses.
No DMARC recordNothing asks receivers to act on fakes. Fix: add p=none with reports, then move up.
p=none for monthsReceivers are asked to do nothing about fakes. Fix: act on the reports, then move to quarantine.
No DKIM key foundUnsigned mail, or a custom selector. Fix: turn on signing in the email service.

Too many DNS lookups

Each include, a, mx, ptr, exists and redirect in an SPF record costs a DNS lookup, and so does each one inside the records it includes. RFC 7208 caps the total at 10. ip4, ip6 and all don’t count.

To get back under, drop services the domain no longer uses, and any ptr, which RFC 7208 says not to publish. Email health counts the record’s own lookups; includes add theirs, so check them when the count nears 10.

A DMARC policy stuck at none

Starting at p=none is right: it’s the time to read reports and fix the domain’s own mail. Left there, it asks receivers to do nothing about mail that fakes the domain.

RFC 9989 warns that p=reject can break mail sent through mailing lists and forwarders, and requires DKIM signing from domains that use it. Before p=reject, it suggests a month or more at p=none, then as long at p=quarantine, comparing reports.

To try a stricter policy, RFC 9989 replaces the old pct= tag with test mode, t=y: receivers then apply one level less, so reject acts as quarantine and quarantine as none. Email health shows both as warnings. Remove pct=, and remove t=y once the reports are clean.

What Google and Yahoo require

Since February 2024, Google and Yahoo have set requirements for mail to their users, with a longer list for bulk senders.

RequirementEvery senderBulk senders
AuthenticationSPF or DKIMSPF and DKIM
DMARCRecommended by bothRequired; p=none is accepted
AlignmentNot requiredFrom aligned with SPF or DKIM
UnsubscribeNot requiredOne-click, plus a visible link
Spam rateBelow 0.3%Below 0.3%
Sending serversForward and reverse DNSThe same
EncryptionTLS, for GmailTLS, for Gmail

Google counts as bulk anyone who sends more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail accounts; Yahoo’s requirements page gives no number. The unsubscribe rules cover marketing and subscribed messages, and Yahoo wants them honoured within 2 days.

Google requires DKIM keys of 1024 bits or longer for personal Gmail accounts and recommends 2048 bits, and Yahoo recommends at least 1024. Email health shows each key’s rough size.

RadiusPilot’s daily limit starts at 50 people and never passes 200, far below Google’s bulk threshold. Google still recommends all three records for every domain: unauthenticated mail might be marked as spam or rejected.

Get your own domain right first

Check yours before you point out anyone else’s.

  1. 1

    List every service that sends as you

    Your mailbox, plus any newsletter, invoicing, booking or form tool using your domain.

  2. 2

    Publish one SPF record

    Include each service, end with ~all, and stay within 10 lookups.

  3. 3

    Turn on DKIM everywhere

    Have each service sign with your own domain, so DKIM aligns.

  4. 4

    Add DMARC in monitoring mode

    v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:reports@yourdomain.ca goes in a TXT record named _dmarc. Reports are built for software, so a report-reading service helps.

  5. 5

    Move up when the reports are clean

    Once every service passes, switch to p=quarantine, then run Email health again.

Turn a weak result into a first email

A weak record is a fact anyone can verify: a fair reason to write, if you stay useful rather than alarming.

  • Lead with one finding, in plain words, and how you found it: public DNS records, nothing sent to their servers.
  • Say what it allows, no more. “Nothing tells mail servers to refuse email that fakes your address” is accurate; “you’ve been hacked” isn’t.
  • Name the fix: a DNS change, by whoever manages the domain or the email.
  • Leave out what you couldn’t confirm, like a DKIM key under a guessed selector.
  • Check again the day you send. Records change.

A first email, in four lines

Hi name, I looked up the public email records for domain.ca. There’s no DMARC record, so nothing tells mail servers to refuse email that fakes a domain.ca address. It’s a small DNS change, and I can set it up or walk your web person through it. Would that help? signature

The rules in Canada

CASL, Canada’s anti-spam law, covers commercial email sent from or opened on a computer in Canada, wherever you’re based. Implied consent can come from an address a business publishes conspicuously, with no statement refusing unsolicited messages, when your email relates to its business.

Each message must identify you, give contact details valid for at least 60 days, and offer an unsubscribe you honour within 10 business days. RadiusPilot adds your business name, postal address and unsubscribe line to every email, blocks your opt-out list and stops at your daily limit.

Published contacts keeps the page where a business published its address, as your record. The email rules guide covers the rest.
