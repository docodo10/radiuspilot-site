Outreach

How to write a first email that names one fact

A method for a first email to a local business: pick one fact the owner can check in a minute, write it in five short parts, and follow CASL and CAN-SPAM.

Google keeps a real spam email on its own help page for site owners. It starts: “Dear google.com, I visited your website and noticed that you are not listed in most of the major search engines and directories…” Right next to it, Google tells owners to “be wary of SEO firms and web consultants or agencies that email you out of the blue.”

That’s the email your prospect has learned to delete: generic, unverifiable, sent to everyone. The way through is one fact, something true about their business that they can check in a minute. This guide shows how to choose it, write around it and stay within the law.

In short

  • One email, one fact the owner can verify in a minute.
  • Check the fact again on the day you send.
  • Five parts: who you are, the fact, how to see it, why it matters, one small next step.
  • No invented numbers, no ranking promises, no fake “Re:”.
  • Identify yourself, offer an unsubscribe and honour it within 10 business days.

Choose one fact

A good fact passes four tests:

  • Verifiable: the owner can see it without your tools or your word.
  • About them: their site, their booking, their listing or their email, not their industry.
  • Current: true today. Websites change, so check again before you send.
  • Fixable by you: something you can do, and do well.
The factHow the owner checks it
The site shows “Not secure”Open it in Chrome and look at the address bar
Booking doesn’t work on a phoneOpen the site on a phone and tap Book
The hours differ between the site and the listingCompare the two side by side
The homepage title is just “Home”Search the business’s name and read the result
No DMARC recordLook the domain up on a public email check
Slow on phonesRun PageSpeed Insights on the Mobile tab

The website audit checklist covers how to find each of these. Before you quote a speed number, read what a good PageSpeed Insights score is. For email records, see SPF, DKIM and DMARC explained.

Then pick the one that matters most to that kind of business: booking for a salon, a working phone link for a plumber. Only one. A list of six problems reads like an attack, and it’s easy to dismiss.

Before you write: where the address came from

In Canada, CASL needs consent for commercial email. Implied consent can come from an address the business published conspicuously, when it wasn’t accompanied by a statement that the person doesn’t want unsolicited messages and when your email is relevant to their business, role, functions or duties.

In practice: take the address from the business’s own website, prefer a role address such as info@, and note the page and the date. Never guess an address or buy a list. Cold email in Canada covers the rules in detail.

In the United States, CAN-SPAM covers business email too: the FTC says the law “makes no exception for business-to-business email”. Its rules for each message are in the footer section below.

Write it in five parts

  1. 1

    Who you are, and why them

    Your name, your business, and one line on why you’re writing to them: you work nearby, or you work with businesses like theirs.

  2. 2

    The fact

    One sentence, in plain words: “Your booking page doesn’t open on a phone.”

  3. 3

    How to see it

    The one-minute check from the table above. It turns your claim into something they can verify, and shows that you looked.

  4. 4

    Why it matters

    What a customer sees or can’t do. No figures about lost sales: you don’t know them, and the owner can tell.

  5. 5

    One small next step

    A ten-minute call, a one-page summary of what you checked, or the fix itself. One ask, easy to say yes to.

A first email, in five parts

Subject: Business: your booking page on phones. Hi name, I’m your name from agency, a web studio in neighbourhood. This morning I tried to book on your website from my phone, and the booking page didn’t open. You can check it the same way: open website on a phone and tap Book. Anyone who books from their phone would hit the same wall. I can show you what I’d change in ten minutes, or send you a one-page summary. Would either help? Signature, business name and postal address Don’t want emails like this? Reply “unsubscribe” and I won’t email you again.

Read it back as the owner would. If a sentence could go to any business, cut it.

An honest subject line

The FTC’s rule is simple: “The subject line must accurately reflect the content of the message.” Google’s sender guidelines say the same about Gmail: “Message headers and message content should be accurate, and not misleading or deceptive.”

  • Name the business and the topic: “Business: your booking page on phones”.
  • No “Re:” or “Fwd:” on a first email. It claims a conversation that never happened.
  • No false urgency, and no question the email doesn’t answer.

What to leave out

  • Numbers you can’t source. No lost sales, no “most customers…”. If a number would help, say how they can measure it.
  • Ranking promises. Google’s own words: “No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google.”
  • Every other problem. Keep them for the call, where you can explain them.
  • Anything you didn’t check yourself, that day.
  • Praise that would fit any business. It reads like the template it is.

The footer the law asks for

For email received in Canada, the Government of Canada’s CASL guidance says every message must include:

  • Your business name, and the name of anyone you’re sending for
  • A current mailing address, plus a phone number, email address or website
  • Contact details that stay valid for at least 60 days after you send
  • An unsubscribe mechanism. Each request must be actioned within 10 business days, at no cost to the recipient

For email to the United States, the FTC’s CAN-SPAM guide asks for accurate header information, a clear and conspicuous disclosure that the message is an advertisement, your valid physical postal address, a clear explanation of how to opt out, and opt-outs honoured within 10 business days.

Gmail adds its own rules. Every sender needs SPF or DKIM on their domain and a spam rate below 0.3% in Postmaster Tools. Above 5,000 messages a day, senders also need DMARC, and marketing messages need one-click unsubscribe. Google also advises starting with a low volume and increasing it slowly.

Follow up, then stop

A short follow-up is fair. A stream of them isn’t. Reply in the same thread, keep it to two or three lines, add one new detail if you have one, and keep the footer. Stop as soon as they reply or unsubscribe, and add every opt-out to your list in every tool you send from.
